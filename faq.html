The ten questions players ask most about bending, answered plainly, with the best free video lessons and the original sources linked under each one.

1What is bending, and why does it work?

Bending is lowering a note's pitch by changing the shape of your mouth and throat while you play. It gives you the bluesy "wail", and it also gives you notes the harmonica doesn't have built in.

Each hole has two reeds, one for blowing and one for drawing. When you reshape your mouth so it resonates near a lower pitch, both reeds start working together and the note slides down toward the lower reed. That's why a normal bend always goes down, and why the size of the gap between a hole's two reeds sets how far it can bend.

All of this happens inside your mouth, so you can't see it from the outside. An MRI study with teacher David Barrett filmed the tongue in action, and it's the clearest look at what's actually happening.

2Which holes bend, and how far?

On a standard 10-hole (Richter-tuned) harmonica, the pattern is the same in every key:

  • Draw bends, holes 1–6: hole 1 bends a half step, hole 2 a whole step, hole 3 a step and a half, hole 4 a half step, hole 6 a half step. Hole 5 barely moves.
  • Blow bends, holes 8–10: hole 8 a half step, hole 9 a half step, hole 10 a whole step. Hole 7 barely moves.

On a C harp, for example, the 3 draw (B) can bend down to B♭, A and A♭. On other keys the actual notes move up or down, but the holes and the number of steps stay the same. The trainer's hole map shows the exact notes for whichever key you pick.

3How do I get my very first bend?

Most people get their first bend on the 4 draw or the 1 draw.

  • Start with a clean single note. Bending a chord or a leaky note is much harder.
  • Keep your lips relaxed and still. Draw gently, then slide your tongue back as if you were saying "ee" and then "oo". You should hear the note dip and come back.
  • Try it without the harmonica first: a falling whistle uses almost the same tongue movement.
  • Expect it to take days or weeks, not minutes. Almost everyone finds it slower than they expected.

4I've tried for ages — why can't I bend at all?

Usually it's one of these:

  • Pulling harder instead of reshaping. Force can drag the pitch a little, but it tires you out, can damage reeds, and doesn't build the real skill.
  • Mouth too open, or the harp too shallow. You need a small, rounded "oo" mouth with a closed-off space behind it. If your lips barely touch the harp, it won't work.
  • Lips doing the work. The lips should only seal. The tongue, jaw and throat do the bending.
  • Tilting the harp. Tilting can help you find your first bend, but it isn't a long-term technique.
  • A very cheap harmonica. Toy-grade harps leak air and make bending much harder (see question 9).

5Why does my 2 draw sound airy, flat, or not sound at all?

This is the most common problem in a player's first years, and the harp usually isn't broken. The normal resting shape of most people's mouths happens to sit close to the pitch of the 2 draw reed, so it half-bends before you've tried to bend anything. The note comes out weak, flat or breathy.

  • Play it softer. A gentle draw often sounds clean where a hard one chokes.
  • Keep the tongue low and flat, with the harp deep in your mouth and a relaxed throat.
  • Keep your nose closed. Breathing through it wastes air and makes bending impossible.
  • Low-key harps (like B♭ and below) make this harder, because their bigger reeds respond more slowly.

6How do I control the three bends on the 3 draw?

The 3 draw has three bends packed into one hole, so the trick is control, not depth. Many players can drop straight to the bottom but can't stop halfway.

  • Learn them one at a time: the half step first, then the whole step, then the step and a half.
  • Different bends use a different tongue position. The 3 draw bends come from raising the middle-to-back of the tongue (you'll feel it near your back molars). The 2 draw needs the hump even further back.
  • Practice in front of a mirror without the harp, then add the harp back.
  • Use a tuner, or the trainer's green target band, to check you're actually landing on the in-between notes.

7How do I know I'm really bending — and in tune?

A real bend makes the note's pitch clearly slide. If you're not sure whether it moved, it probably didn't yet. Changes in tone or volume alone don't count.

Being in tune is the next step. A bend that only gets three-quarters of the way down sounds sour against a band. Practice two skills:

  • Slides: start on the natural note and glide down into the bent note.
  • Straight hits: land directly on the bent note with no audible slide.

8Does the key of my harmonica change how it bends?

The holes and bend sizes are the same on every key, but how it feels changes a lot:

  • Lower keys (G, A, B♭, and "low" harps) have bigger reeds. Bends are deep and loose but respond slowly, so they're easy to overshoot. The 2 draw is noticeably harder on a B♭ than on a C.
  • Higher keys (E, F, F♯) have small, stiff reeds. Bends happen in a tight window and need precise tongue moves.
  • C, D and A sit in the middle, which is why they're the usual keys to learn bending on.

Range matters too. Standard harps in G to B start in a lower octave than harps in C to F♯, so a G harp is lower than a C harp, not higher.

A handy trick from experienced players: if you can bend a certain pitch on one harp, you can find the same pitch on another key and use the same mouth shape.

9Is it me, or is my harmonica the problem?

Most of the time it's technique, but the harp can make things much harder:

  • Very cheap or toy harps leak air and have poorly set reeds. A solid entry-level harp (the kind most teachers recommend) bends far more easily.
  • Reed gaps (how far each reed sits from its slot) affect how a hole bends. A badly gapped reed can feel stuck, or jump straight to the bottom of the bend.
  • Test it: if one hole fails on one harp but the same note bends fine on a different harp, suspect the harp.

Reed gapping is a skill you can learn, or a harmonica technician can set a harp up for you.

10What are blow bends, overblows and overdraws?
  • Blow bends (holes 8–10) work like draw bends, but while blowing. They need a narrower, more focused airstream. Learn them after your draw bends feel solid.
  • Overblows (holes 1, 4, 5, 6) and overdraws (holes 7, 9, 10) raise the pitch. The opposite reed pops in and plays a note just above the normal one, filling the last gaps in the scale.

Overblows are an advanced technique. Most players start with the 6 overblow, and a well-gapped harp helps a lot. The trainer includes them under "Show overblows & overdraws".
